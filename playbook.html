The Hook Playbook

9 tricks that keep you on the app

None of these are secret, and falling for them doesn’t mean you’re weak-willed. They use ordinary human psychology — the same pull as a cliffhanger. Apps just use all of them at once, all day.

First: why do apps do this?

Your attention pays the bills

Instagram and YouTube are free to use because they make most of their money from ads. More time on the app means more ads seen — and more information about what keeps you watching.

  1. 1. Trigger A buzz, or plain habit, gets you to open the app.
  2. 2. Action You scroll, tap or swipe. It takes almost no effort.
  3. 3. Variable reward Sometimes something great, sometimes nothing. You can’t tell which.
  4. 4. Investment You like, post, follow or subscribe — so next time there’s more to come back for.

This loop is the Hook Model, from Nir Eyal’s book for app designers, Hooked. Then it starts again.

The 9 tricks

Spot them, name them

Each one comes with a real example from Instagram or YouTube, why it works, and your move.

01

Variable rewards

You never know what the next swipe will bring — something boring, or something great. Because the good stuff turns up at random, you keep checking. Slot machines work the same way.

On Instagram
The feed, Reels, pull-to-refresh
On YouTube
Shorts, pull-to-refresh on Home
Why it works
Rewards we can’t predict keep us checking more than rewards we can. Nir Eyal’s Hook Model calls them “variable rewards”.
Your move
Give the slot machine a closing time: a Shorts feed limit or an Instagram daily limit.

Try the pull-to-refresh demo

02

No stopping cues

Books have chapters. Episodes end. Natural endings give you a moment to decide whether to carry on. Apps remove them: the feed never runs out, and the next video starts by itself.

On Instagram
Infinite scroll, Reels
On YouTube
Shorts, autoplay
Why it works
Without an ending, stopping becomes a decision you have to make on your own, again and again. In a 2025 study, switching off Netflix’s autoplay cut how long people watched, per day and per sitting (Schaffner and others).
Your move
Put a stopping point back: turn off autoplay and set limits.

03

Social validation

Likes, views, followers and comments work like little scores. Getting them feels good, so we post, check, and post again.

On Instagram
Likes, view counts, followers, comments
On YouTube
Views, likes, subscriber counts, comments
Why it works
In a brain-scan study, teens looked at photos in an Instagram-style app. When a photo seemed to have lots of likes, reward areas of their brains were more active — and they were more likely to like it too (Sherman and others, 2016).
Your move
Hide like counts — on other people’s posts and your own.

Watch likes trickle in

04

Personalisation

The app notices what you watch, rewatch, like or skip, and serves more of whatever keeps you there. The better it knows you, the harder it is to put down.

On Instagram
Explore, the Reels feed
On YouTube
Home, Up next
Why it works
It’s very good at predicting what you’ll watch next. In 2018, YouTube said about 70% of watch time came from its recommendations.
Your move
Reset your Instagram suggestions or pause your YouTube watch history. Curious how it works? The algorithm, explained.
05

Triggers

A buzz, a badge or a red dot is a tap on the shoulder. It pulls you back into the app — and once you’re in, it’s easy to stay.

On Instagram
Notifications, red badges
On YouTube
The bell, notifications
Why it works
The Hook Model starts with a trigger: the cue that gets you to open the app. In our survey, 16 of 21 students had opened an app because of a notification and stayed longer than they meant to.
Your move
Mute app notifications, keep messages from people: Instagram, YouTube.
06

FOMO and scarcity

Things that vanish make you check now. Stories disappear after 24 hours, and a live only happens once. Wait, and you miss it.

On Instagram
24-hour Stories, live alerts
On YouTube
Live alerts
Why it works
Anything that might disappear feels urgent, so checking now feels safer than checking later. Researchers link features like this to the fear of missing out (Montag and others, 2019).
Your move
Mute Stories from accounts you only check out of habit.
07

Loss aversion and investment

Once you’ve built something — followers, posts, subscriptions, a feed that “gets” you — leaving feels like losing it. So you keep coming back to look after it.

On Instagram
Followers, your posts
On YouTube
Subscriptions, playlists, your channel
Why it works
We value things more once they’re ours — psychologists call it the endowment effect (Montag and others, 2019). The Hook Model calls the effort you put in “investment”.
Your move
Just notice it. A week of fewer check-ins doesn’t delete anything: your followers and subscriptions will still be there.
08

Unfinished tasks

Unfinished things nag at us. A row of unwatched Stories, half a playlist, a queue of “Up next” — your brain wants to close the loop.

On Instagram
Rings around unwatched Stories
On YouTube
Playlists, the Up next queue
Why it works
It’s called the Zeigarnik effect: we remember, and return to, unfinished tasks more than finished ones (Montag and others, 2019).
Your move
Turn off autoplay so the queue can’t run itself, and mute Stories you don’t care about so fewer rings nag.
09

Social pressure

When an app shows you’ve “Seen” a message, you feel you owe a reply. Comments and tags work the same way: someone is waiting for you.

On Instagram
“Seen” in DMs, comments, tags
On YouTube
Replies and mentions in comments
Why it works
Read receipts are a kind of social pressure: once someone knows you’ve read their message, not answering feels rude (Montag and others, 2019).
Your move
Turn off read receipts for one chat, or for all of them.

Take this with you

The three-question test

Use it on any feature, in any app — including this website.

  1. Who benefits? You, the app, or both?
  2. Did I choose this? Or was it switched on for me?
  3. Can I stop when I want? Is there a natural end, or do I have to fight my way out?

Try it on

YouTube autoplay

Who benefits?
Mostly the app: more watch time means more ads.
Did I choose this?
Probably not: if your account says you’re 18 or over, it starts switched on.
Can I stop when I want?
Only if you beat the countdown.

Verdict: a hook. Worth switching off.

Try it on

Instagram Sleep mode

Who benefits?
You: quieter nights.
Did I choose this?
You can — and on teen accounts it’s on from 10 pm to 7 am.
Can I stop when I want?
Yes, any time.

Verdict: a tool that works for you. Keep it.
